Making of My Website Part 1 - Cookie Consent Game

Many of you know that I'm usually up for quite some fun when I decide to build something new, because I don't like people acting professionally when in fact they're just faking it until they make it. I'm okay being a human that's too stupid to write programs, and I'm okay being a human that's too stupid to design UIs. The least I can do is have some fun while doing it.

The idea to redesign my website has been long overdue. My blogging system was not fun to use, and made writing articles quite painful. I already chose to use markdown as a format which was occasionally helpful, but still, I usually give up when I'm 80% into writing the article because I doubt my quality of research about a specific topic that I want to write about.

Inspiration

The last redesign of my website was 7 years ago (in 2018 but oh boi has the internet changed since then). It's almost Christmas, so why not use the time before the holidays to try to enjoy building web applications a little?

Couldn't resist that snarky comment about web development. For my website I decided to ditch all web bundler and transpilation tools, I don't give a damn about performance ratings of Google's Web Console. This website is made to share knowledge and to share the insights on how to build stuff, because that's how I learned how to build websites as a teenager and I want to give the next generation of developers the same kind of experience.

Consent Game

There was some kind stranger a couple years ago that gave me the idea for a cookie consent game. I even looked it up in my git history to dig out the their comment on Algolia .

Reading that comment makes me realize that the idea stuck with me eversince. At the time, I was still hurt by what the FSB did to me in 2021 , and I was trying to stay as anon as humanly possible. So I kind of ditched a lot of interaction where I couldn't guarantee my operational security and safety of the people I have left to care about.

So if you're reading this, kind stranger, thanks for that idea!

I was taking a look at an old Game Prototype I built for a Hackathon, called Cosmo which was built with my previous efforts of bringing game development methodologies to the React and software development world. Turns out, that was an attempt made for failure from the start.

Some of the game assets I used in Cosmo at the time were from Kenney, who is also known as Asset Jesus because he provided free assets in his Twitch Streams where on the weekend. He took the time to make assets for people watching his stream. All licensed under the CC license.

I wanted to build a fun space shooter, so I took the liberty to combine some of the assets that he provided in these asset packs:

Game Theme

I roughly knew what to make the game about:

  • Cookies , lots of them
  • Explosions , lots of them
  • Particles , lots of them
  • Space Shooter , maybe an R-Type like sidescroller

But I still lacked a matching game theme that would be fun.

I was thinking about this a lot, and had to come back to the defamation attempt of the 4chan kids back at the time. There was one funny picture that stuck with me and that I still had on my hard drive, where they were portraying me as the Emperor Palpatine of cookies, describing me as a glowie and making me the living embodiment of their spy agency enemies.

I kind of love that picture because I can take it with a bit of humor. I don't give a damn about my perception online, so this theme was the ideal candidate for a little more self humor. I don't understand humans and their emotions much, so why not use this excuse to find out how much I can lean into the theme?

In my mind this dark theme combined with some Star Wars Empire and Sith flair could be really nice. I was just missing some final touch for it. I actually watched a lot of voice acting classes and a lot of audio editing videos, and even took an online course about voice acting and voice editing to figure out whether it's possible to modify my voice to sound like Palpatine.

And I actually spent tons of time recording my voice in a dark overtoned manner. Way too much time and way too annoying for my neighbors.

TacoTRON

Then I remembered TacoTRON . When it came out it as mindblowing how well it was able to clone voices to sound similar to a speech sample that you provided. Turns out, it really is good at cloning my voice into Darth Sidious.

Getting TacoTRON to run is actually quite easy. NVIDIA even provides some Speech Synthesis Tools in their DeepLearningExamples repository on GitHub. It's somewhat maintained, but it's better to run it directly in Podman on an Ubuntu LTS container. You know, because Python environments are always messed up, no matter how much you try to fix them.

I also read about the training behaviors of TacoTRON, and I really liked Ben Sherman's article and previous work with TacoTRON so I'm giving credit where credit is due:

  • NVIDIA's TacoTRON examples
  • Blog Post about TacoTRON for TTS
  • Ben Sherman's TacoTRON fork

Transcribing the movie scenes was the most painful part, where I spent two days just watching, re-watching, editing, and cutting out the scenes into separate audio streams. That work took a lot of the initial excitement, but after two days on the weekend I was able to let TacoTRON run on the speech samples. Training time on my shitty selfhosted setup took another day, but then it was finally ready to try it out.

Here are the first speech samples that I generated using TacoTRON and the cloned voice of Emperor Palpatine . I converted them to mp3 for the sake of saving some bandwidth, because wav isn't really a great format for the web.

Asset Loading

In my case, for both the Avatar and the Game itself, I implemented a common library of sorts that manages the caching parts of decoding all assets into the formats I have to use internally, so that I don't have to deal with cache issues anywhere else in the code.

I also returned a Promise for all loaders so that I can use Promise.all() to have more advanced chains of loading flows that can be combined across codebases. The cache mechanism works transparently, so that redundant loading calls of assets will lead to just returning the already cached buffers.

This saved a lot of the audio codec support headaches, because the supported codec detection was implemented right into the loaders themselves. I also used a base URL instance everywhere so that the files could be included from other relative paths while still preserving the correct absolute URL s to the assets themselves. 

// In part from game/Init.mjs
import { LoadImage  } from "/design/consent/common/image/LoadImage.mjs";
import { LoadMusic  } from "/design/consent/common/music/LoadMusic.mjs";
import { LoadSound  } from "/design/consent/common/sound/LoadSound.mjs";
import { LoadSpeech } from "/design/consent/common/speech/LoadSpeech.mjs";

export const Init = (avatar, wrapper) => {

	const base = new URL(".", import.meta.url);

	return new Promise((resolve, reject) => {

		return Promise.all([

			LoadImage("Cookie",       new URL("./entities/Cookie.png", base)),
			LoadMusic("game-boss",    [ new URL("./music/game-boss.m4a", base) /* ... */ ]),
			LoadSound("lazer",        [ new URL("./sound/lazer.m4a", base)     /* ... */ ]),
			LoadSpeech("game-level1", new URL("./speeches/no-consent.json", base), [ new URL("./speeches/no-consent.m4a", base) /* ... */ ]),
			// etc pp.

		]).then(() => {
			let game = new Game(/* ... */);
			resolve(game);
		}).catch((err) => {
			reject(err);
		});

	});

};

AudioContext API

When trying to find out how to manipulate Audio on the Web, it took me a while to understand the graph concept behind the Web Audio API .

My last days of dealing with Web Audio was when iOS was too stupid to play more than one audio stream at the time, because WebKit was using the iTunes app in the background to play audio files in the web browser. At the time we also filed a lot of Use after free bugs, which hopefully triggered a refactor of that part of the codebase. Personally, I removed all that legacy platform code in my RetroKit fork.

Anyways, kudos to the authors of the Web Audio API . Once you get used to everything being nodes that have to be connected once and are automatically garbage collected after they've ended, it's a really nice concept to deal with AudioBuffer instances and other node types to implement sound effects and filters on top of it.

I was even able to implement positional audio very quickly, so that when you shoot lazers on the left side, they will be heard from the left side. 

// From common/sound/PlaySoundAt.mjs
import { audio_context } from "./audio_context.mjs";
import { sound_buffers } from "./sound_buffers.mjs";

export const PlaySoundAt = (name, position, callback) => {

	position = typeof position === "number"   ? position : 0.0;
	callback = typeof callback === "function" ? callback : null;

	if (position > 1.0) {
		position = 1.0;
	}

	if (position < -1.0) {
		position = -1.0;
	}

	let buffer = sound_buffers[name] || null;
	if (buffer !== null) {

		let source = audio_context.createBufferSource();
		let panner = audio_context.createStereoPanner();

		source.buffer = buffer;
		panner.pan.value = position;

		source.connect(panner).connect(audio_context.destination);

		if (callback !== null) {
			source.onended = () => {
				callback();
			};
		}

		source.start();

	}

};

Canvas 2D API

For rendering, I decided to go with the Canvas 2D API . Little did I know at the time how many context.save() and context.restore() calls I would need in order to make all the game logic and collisions happen correctly.

For example, at some point I made the Lazer entity directional, meaning that the length property and the speed vector would essentially decide how the lazer can collide with other entities, which is different from the typical AABB or radius based collisions.

I had to implement that, because a bounding box wouldn't work when the lazer was very long and the enemy was in a diagonal firing position towards the player's spaceship. Took me a bit to realize what was going on when I started to fight the game's Star Destroyer Cookie boss and I wanted it to fire a huge lazer as a mechanism of attacking the player and making the fight a little harder to survive.

As you can see, the bounding box of the lazer is different than its rendering, because the width and height aren't rotated correctly. This angle of the lazer led to the necessary context.save() and context.restore() calls to be able to transform the coordinate system correctly. The Lazer's CollidesWith(entity) method got way more expensive to compute, too. 

// In part from game/entities/Lazer.mjs
Lazer.prototype.Render = function(ctx, delta) {

	let rad    = this.angle * Math.PI / 180;
	let length = this.scale * this.height;

	ctx.save();
	ctx.translate(this.position.x, this.position.y);
	ctx.rotate(rad + Math.PI / 2);
	ctx.globalAlpha = 1.0;
	ctx.fillStyle = "#ff0f5a";
	ctx.fillRect(
		-this.width  / 2,
		0,
		this.width,
		length
	);
	ctx.globalAlpha = 1.0;

	ctx.restore();

};

Juice It or Lose It

When I was still a gamedev at Zynga, there was this famous talk going around, which we showed to all newbies that had no idea how to polish their game prototypes.

The talk was called Juice It or Lose It by Martin Jonasson and Petri Purho. They have a nice Indie Game Studio called grapefrukt which I recommend to check out.

Anyways, the talk made a simple Paddle game that was bouncing a ball back and forth towards the stone wall, and they gradually polished it with more effects that they added incrementally.

Essentially they categorized these into types of effects:

  • Tweening and Animation curves (e.g. EaseOut , BounceEaseOut etc)
  • Color effects on interaction
  • Bounce and screen shaking effects
  • Sound effects on interaction
  • Particle effects
  • Music for setting the theme of the game
  • Avatar customizations (e.g. making things emotionally pleasing)

In my Consent Game case, I can't make it cute and wholesome. People need to be able to put their anger and frustration with the world of cookie banners into pure hatred and destruction! The Galactic Cookie Empire therefore deserves a dark and mysterious music theme, and the game must feel like it's fear and rising terror of being tracked any second.

So it was pretty obvious that I would go for lots of explosions and an action-rich vibe here, and lots of lazers.

The explosions were actually quite hard to implement and to get right, given that I wanted to avoid having to deal with huge asset file sizes on the web. I tried some effects like using different blending techniques that change the alpha values, but pretty much all of them were computationally so expensive that my laptop almost died while trying them out.

In the end I decided on a simple particle system where the explosions are rendered as lots of sprites, and the particle's lifetime decides how the ctx.globalAlpha is set, and added a bunch of randomization when it comes to direction and speed vectors, which were just a simple circle. The explosion image itself is just a radial gradient that goes from yellow on the inside to red on the outside, so that it looks more like the color scheme of a fire. 

// In part from game/entities/Explosion.mjs
import { GetImage } from "../../common/image/GetImage.mjs";

export const Explosion = function(x, y) {

	this.image = GetImage("Explosion");

	this.life      = 1.0;
	this.particles = [];

	let amount  = 10 + (Math.random() * 10);
	let maxlife = 0.0;

	for (let p = 0; p < amount; p++) {

		let angle = Math.random() * Math.PI * 2;
		let speed = 10 + Math.random() * 30;
		let life  = Math.random() * 1.0;

		if (life > maxlife) {
			maxlife = life;
		}

		this.particles.push({
			life: life,
			r:    (16 + Math.random() * 32) | 0,
			x:    x,
			y:    y,
			vx:   Math.cos(angle) * speed,
			vy:   Math.sin(angle) * speed
		});

	}

	this.life = maxlife;

};

Game Over Dialog

I wanted the game to be a little more challenging, so I implemented an additional secret level that players can play when they're good enough in the statistical results sense.

The statistical values of actually completing a level good enough to unlock the secret level, I decided to go for a percentage threshold. 

// ex gratia
let level1_5_percent = (statistics.level1.spawned * 0.05); // > 95%
let level2_3_percent = (statistics.level2.spawned * 0.03); // > 97%
let level3_1_percent = (statistics.level3.spawned * 0.01); // > 99%

For the Game Over Dialog itself I created matching award pictures using a simple Stable Diffusion approach with funny cookie-themed prompts so that the awards look similar. Getting Stable Diffusion to use the same Seed was quite hard, as it's really weird how some words in the prompt trigger it into abandoning the Seed completely. Somehow anything that's remotely correlated to book will abandon the Seed, always. Very weird.

DOM Shenanigans

The Game itself runs on its own canvas element in the background, as I wanted to preserve a smooth animation of the figure#avatar to the top left when the game is started. In order to understand what's going on, we have to take a look at the DOM structure first: 

<body data-layout="index">
	<header>...</header>
	<div>
		<aside class="visible">...</aside>
		<main>
			<figure id="avatar">...</figure>
            <!-- Other Website Content -->
			<article id="results">...</article>
		</main>
	</div>
	<!-- This is where the Consent Game goes -->
	<canvas id="consent-game"></canvas>
</body>

When the game starts, it sets a body[data-mode] property that influences the rest of the content by using CSS transitions, where it essentially uses transform: translate(x, y) on the remaining layout elements like aside , header , and footer . The remaining nested article elements inside the main are the siblings of the figure#avatar element, and therefore had to be removed from the DOM by using display: none on them.

Note that the stylesheets for the aside and main are dependent on the aside.visible + main selector to give the main element its left offset depending on the aside.visible state and width of that element. 

body[data-mode="game"] {
	width: 100%; height: 100%;
	background: #000000;
	overflow: hidden;
	transition: all 250ms ease-out;
}

body[data-mode="game"] canvas#consent-game {
	display: block;
	position: fixed;
	top: 0px; right: 0px; bottom: 0px; left: 0px;
	background: transparent;
	z-index: 100;
}

/* Similar transform transitions for aside and footer */
body[data-mode="game"] > header {
	transform: translate(0%, -100%);
	transition: transform 250ms ease-out;
}

body[data-mode="game"] > div > aside.visible + main,
body[data-mode="game"] > div > main {
	position: absolute;
	width: 100%; height: 100%;
	top: 0px; right: 0px; bottom: 0px; left: 0px;
	overflow: hidden;
	user-select: none;
	pointer-events: none;
	background: transparent;
	z-index: 101;
}

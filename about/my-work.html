About My Work

Network Security

(or Where Packets Go to Confess Their Sins )

I possess a concerningly intimate understanding of network protocols, their specifications, their implementation quirks, and the fascinating ways they betray their designers under pressure. Over time, I have developed an appreciation for the many creative methods by which protocols almost do what they were intended to do.

This includes an unreasonable familiarity with TCP , UDP , MQTT, ICMP , SNMP , SOCKS, TLS , and network protocols related to the Web such as HTTP/S , SPDY, QUIC, DNS , DoH , DoT , DNS-SD , WebRTC , and WS13 . I know them not just as standards, but as living systems with personalities, habits, and deeply held grudges.

Much of my open-source work lives in the peer-to-peer realm, where software must survive in hostile environments filled with NATs, firewalls, and network administrators who believe that blocking everything is a viable security strategy. These systems employ NAT traversal, relay networks, multicast discovery, and techniques that look suspiciously like malware - mostly because modern malware has become very good at networking.

This sometimes results in software that behaves like a well-intentioned spy : polite, efficient, and extremely difficult to block. Basically like a British person.

Cyber Threat Intelligence

(or How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Automate the Investigation )

Over the years, I've spent a considerable amount of time attempting to automate tasks that traditionally required human intuition, coffee, and a strong emotional tolerance for bad data. Much of my work revolves around transforming OSINT workflows into systems that can observe, correlate, and infer without burning out or asking philosophical questions at 3 AM.

Projects like Research and Tholian Stealth were early attempts at teaching machines how to investigate the internet without accidentially joining a botnet union or starting a small international incident.

On the defensive side, I've built systems like Tholian Firewall and Tholian Detective , which aim to translate threat intelligence into actionable defense mechanisms. These tools observe patterns in vulnerabilities, attacker infrastructure, and behavioral signatures in order to answer the eternal question : Is this malicious, or just badly written code?

Smaller projects like Antibote and Antispam focus on mapping the strange ecosystems of phishing campaigns, spam operations, and botnets by tracing their infrastructure, correlating their ASN data, and gently shining a bright flashlight into places that would rather remain in the darkness.

Purpleteaming

(or Because Red and Blue were Never Enough Headache for Everyone )

My background in both Red and Blue Teaming eventually led to the only reasonable conclusion : The separation was artificial, inefficient, and mildly theatrical.

As a software engineer, I strongly believe in test-driven development. Not just for code, but for security itself. If a vulnerability can't be reliably tested, reproduced, and verified, then it's not really understood. In my view, security controls should be treated like unit tests for reality.

This philosophy naturally led me toward Purple Teaming, where offense and defense stop pretending to be enemies and instead collaborate to make systems measurably more resilient. The goal isn't to win an exercise, it's to reduce the number of surprises at 3 AM for all parties involved.

This mindset also explains my fondness for Go . It is a language that values clarity, simplicity, and low operational friction. And it's, very coincidentally , good at producing fast, portable malware for both defensive and offensive research. It also plays remarkably well with lower-level code via CGo and eBPF , which allows me to combine safety with just enough danger to stay interesting.

Cyber Wiki

(or How I Stopped Worrying and Started Archiving the Internet )

I've participated in more CTFs and hackathons than I can reasonably justify, sometimes as a competitor, sometimes as an organizer, and occasionally as the person asking "Are we sure this challenge won't accidentally summon something?"

Over time, it became increasingly clear that knowledge on the internet is alarmingly fragile. Entire communities, research efforts, and archives vanish quietly every year, taking their hard-earned expertise with them. This is not ideal.

As a result I've been working on my Cyber Wiki , a peer-to-peer self-hostable knowledge system designed to survive link rot, corporate abandonment, and the slow decay of institutional memory. It's meant to be a place where information can persist, even if the misinformation apocalypse doesn't want it to.

Projects like ZIMdex aim to make this content portable and compatible with existing archival formats, because knowledge shouldn't disappear just because a website forgot to renew its domain.

Cyber Warfare

(or A Brief Meditation on the Modern Battlefield )

Censorship resistance used to be a noble technical challenge. Unfortunately, it has since been enthusiastically adopted by people who mistake being loud for being right. Meanwhile, foreign state actors have learned that destabilizing societies is cheaper and more effective than conventional warfare.

In the modern era, cyber warfare is not about tanks or missiles. It is about narratives, influence, confusion, and attention. It is a war of perception, where the objective is not conquest but erosion. Of trust, of truth, and of shared reality.

In this sense, information warfare has become a kind of meta-conflict. One where controlling the story matters more than controlling the territory.

To summarize this philosophy, I offer the following excerpt, inspired by Sun Tzu's The Art of War , rewritten for a slightly more digital age. 

In ancient times, Heaven governed the winds and storms, and Earth shaped the terrain of battle.
Today, a new realm lies between them - the Shadow Domain,
neither seen nor touched, yet shaping it all.

In the Shadow Domain, armies have no form, soldiers bear no banners, and weapons leave no sound.
A single force may stir the clouds, crack the ground, and whisper into the minds of men.

To strike the Shadow Domain is to darken the skies of an enemy's judgement,
to flood his roads with confusion and despair,
and to unsettle his people without crossing his borders.

He who commands this realm commands not just the battlefield,
but the thoughts, tools, and time of his opponent.

Thus, the supreme strategist does not merely wield force - he shapes perception.
He does not seize territory - he seizes certainty.

And when certainty is taken, even the strongest of nations may stumble in daylight
as though they were walking through the mist.

- Sun Cookie
